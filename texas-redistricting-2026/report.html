What would the 2026 House vote look like under Texas’s old map?

The downloads and initial reconstruction are ready. Eight old districts have provisional estimates. Thirty remain pending because the available records do not yet support a complete reconstruction.

The statewide seat effect is not determined. The observed new-map result has 24 Republican and 14 Democratic leads. The old-map estimates below cover only part of the state and cannot be compared with that full-state seat count.

Do precincts stay the same?

Sometimes. Counties can redraw, split, merge, or renumber voting precincts independently of congressional districts. A voter can also keep the same precinct while changing congressional district. We therefore need the precinct boundaries used for the election whose votes we are counting. The legislature publishes separate precinct vintages.

Even if a 2026 precinct is wholly inside a new district, an old district boundary can cut through it. A precinct vote total alone does not reveal how each side voted. Smaller reporting units or ballot-style records may resolve the split; otherwise it requires an estimate.

What was calculated

  1. Downloaded the official old and new PLANC2193 and PLANC2333 shapefiles and their Census-block assignments.
  2. Matched the same 668,757 Census blocks across both plans. District numbers changed for blocks in 73 counties. Block counts are geographic bookkeeping, not voter or population counts.
  3. Downloaded official 2026 House returns for every county. All candidate totals sum exactly to the state totals for all 38 districts and agree with the state's separate HTML summary.
  4. Downloaded precinct returns for Bastrop, Bexar, Denton, Harris, Tarrant, Travis, and Williamson. Only county/district/party cells matching the state snapshot exactly enter the precinct reconstruction. Unmatched cells retain county-level uncertainty; this includes Bexar’s major-party returns.
  5. Assigned a whole reporting unit directly when it belongs to one old district. For split precincts, used 2020 Census voting-age population, with total population and land area as sensitivity checks. Each populated Census block is placed in a precinct using a point inside the block; its old and new congressional districts come from the official block assignments. This is an allocation model, not measured within-precinct voting.
  6. Left districts missing major-party precinct data pending, as requested. No statewide model fills those gaps, and no 2024 votes substitute for 2026 votes.

The estimate assigns 6,236,936 votes to old districts and retains 3,543,118 votes without an old-district allocation. Their sum is exactly 9,780,054 in each scenario. Some districts with an allocation still remain pending because not all their major-party votes are located.

What remains uncertain

  • Precinct vintage: the legislature’s latest published boundaries are from the 2026 primary. They need confirmation against the November election. Congress/precinct geometry conflicts affecting 79 accepted votes are left unresolved.
  • Special precinct labels: 412 votes have source labels absent from the shapefile, including F99 suffixes. The suffixes were not stripped to invent a geographic match.
  • Population allocation: 2020 residents, including voting-age residents, are not the 2026 electorate. New construction, turnout, and within-precinct party differences can matter. About 2,371 split-unit votes require an area fallback where the available population crosswalk supplies no weight.
  • Incomplete precinct collection: the downloaded and reconciled files cover only part of the necessary counties. Some other reports may be available but have not been obtained and adapted here. Missing data is not assumed to be zero.
  • Counterfactual behavior: an election actually held under old boundaries could have different candidates, campaigns, turnout, and voter choices. This exercise cannot recover those changes.
