What the results show

Skipping is common. Motive is harder to establish.

Unopposed judges receive far fewer votes than contested judges. Precincts that favor the other party skip them more often. That relationship appears throughout the period, so the pattern by itself does not establish an unusual protest in 2026.

The party pattern is persistent

For unopposed local trial judges, the mean precinct correlation between skipping and opposing-party support is 0.990 in 2026. Across earlier observed elections it ranges from 0.986 to 0.994. A stronger opposing-party precinct is consistently more likely to leave these races blank.

The raw increase overstates the change

Unopposed trial-court skipping rose from 36.7% in 2024 to 47.2% in 2026. Holding precinct Senate partisanship at a modeled 50–50, the average fitted rate changes from 36.6% to 38.7%. This is a descriptive adjustment; different judges and electorates still matter.

What we can conclude: the data supports party-related skipping. It cannot identify which individual voters skipped, whether they were angry, or why. Unopposed Republican judges also receive substantial net support beyond Republican top-of-ticket totals.

What this tests

If party affiliation is connected to skipping, unopposed Republican judges should have higher blank rates in Democratic precincts. The upward pattern is clear.

In 2026, an extra 10 percentage points of Democratic Senate support is associated with about 8.7 points more skipping, averaging separate ballot-weighted fits across the 19 unopposed trial races.

That is consistent with voters declining to endorse the other party. It is also consistent with routine partisan voting rather than anger. Candidates running unopposed can receive votes beyond their party’s top-race total while still being skipped by many voters.

Why party-adjusted comparisons matter: when more voters choose Democrats at the top, an unopposed Republican can have a higher blank rate even if voters’ behavior at a given precinct party mix barely changes.

The 2024-to-2026 fitted comparison above uses Senate in both years. It does not join precincts across years or estimate individual-level behavior. Race-level correlations are summarized descriptively; repeated races share many of the same ballots. Every observed unopposed countywide judicial candidate in this period was Republican, so an equivalent comparison with Democratic countywide judges is unavailable. Democratic JP races cover different electorates.

Methods & sources

How to reproduce the report

The source trail starts with the county’s past-election archive. Every even-year November election from 2010 through 2024 provides a tally export formatted as CSV, sometimes inside a ZIP. The 2026 county ZIP was still a placeholder, so this report uses Clarity’s versioned summary CSV and precinct XML instead.

Definitions and scope
  • Judicial races: Supreme Court, Court of Criminal Appeals, regional appellate courts, district and family courts, and county trial/probate courts. Only votes cast in Tarrant are counted. These are countywide in coverage, although appellate districts extend beyond the county.
  • Separate groups: justices of the peace cover subsets of the county. The elected County Judge is the county executive and is reported separately so it does not stand in for trial judges.
  • Uncontested: exactly one named candidate, counting declared write-ins as candidates even with zero votes. Rejected/unassigned write-in buckets are not candidates. Contested means two or more named candidates.
  • Blank rate: reported undervotes divided by eligible ballots. A reported undervote indicates no valid choice for a vote-for-one contest, not the voter’s reason. Overvotes and invalid-vote buckets remain separate.
  • Vote retention: named judicial votes divided by named benchmark votes in identical reporting units. Vote drop is one minus that ratio. This differs from blank rate. Both comparisons are provided.
  • Aggregation: ballot/undervote fields repeat on candidate rows. They are counted once per contest and reporting unit. Summary rates pool ballot opportunities across selected races. A single person may contribute to many opportunities.
  • Eligibility: federal-only limited ballots can appear in Senate totals but not local totals. Exact reporting-unit matching controls this. Each judicial race retains its own eligible-ballot denominator.
Two legal changes interrupt the trend

The straight-ticket voting option ended in 2020. Before then a party selection could cast votes for multiple judicial candidates. See the Texas Secretary of State’s 2020 advisory.

In 2022, 29 judicial offices serving Tarrant were declared elected without a vote: 23 local trial seats, one statewide appellate seat, and five JP seats. Their blank rates are unavailable. The county certification and state certification identify these offices. The rule was repealed before 2024, as explained in the 2024 Secretary of State advisory.

Precinct analysis and limits on interpretation

Splits are combined within each election. For each unopposed race and each benchmark, the analysis fits a ballot-weighted line relating the blank rate to opposing-party share of the Republican-plus-Democratic benchmark vote. Precincts need at least 100 ballots and 50 major-party benchmark votes. The average fitted rate at 50% opposing-party support is a descriptive adjustment for partisan composition. It is not a matched-panel design or a causal estimate.

The same voters appear in several races, judges and ballot positions change, and turnout and precinct boundaries differ. No statistical significance claim treats these races as independent. Aggregate data cannot connect an individual’s top-race choice to a skipped judge, and cannot measure anger. A recent cast-vote-record analysis could directly measure the joint voting pattern, but still would not establish motive.
