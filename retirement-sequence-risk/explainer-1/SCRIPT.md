# 8% or 4%? The order of returns decides: script for approval

The source of truth is [narration.json](narration.json). This file is a readable copy with what's on screen during each line. Voiced with Kokoro (Heart) through Voicebox; about 5½ minutes.


## 1. The 8% promise

> Dave Ramsey tells retirees they can spend eight percent of their savings every year.

*On screen:* Title: “Spend 8% of your savings, every year, forever?”

> His math is simple. Stocks average twelve percent. Inflation takes four. That leaves eight.

*On screen:* 12% − 4% = 8% builds up, one term at a time

> Most retirement research lands closer to four percent, half the income.

*On screen:* Two pills: Ramsey 8%, research about 4%

> So let's test both rules against a hundred and fifty years of real stock market history.

*On screen:* “1872–2025 · 154 years of S&P 500 returns”


## 2. Ramsey's world

> First, the world Ramsey describes. The market returns exactly twelve percent, every single year.

*On screen:* Identical +12% bars fill a strip along the bottom

> Retire with a million dollars, take eighty thousand, and raise it with inflation each year.

*On screen:* $1M balance line draws over 30 years, with the label “$80,000 a year, raised with inflation”

> Even here the balance slowly leaks. Twelve percent growth with four percent inflation is really seven point seven percent, not eight.

*On screen:* Year-30 dot: $671K left. “1.12 ÷ 1.04 = 7.7% real growth, not 8%”

> But it's close. The money would last forty-four years. The trouble is that real markets don't look like this.

*On screen:* The line continues to zero: “Runs out in year 44”


## 3. Real years aren't average

> Here is every year of the S&P 500 since 1872, with dividends.

*On screen:* All 154 annual returns grow in as bars, blue up and red down

> Only twelve of those hundred and fifty-four years landed within two points of twelve percent.

*On screen:* A 10–14% band highlights 12 bars; counter “12 of 154 years”

> Forty-one of them lost money. Three lost a third or more.

*On screen:* Losing years highlighted; counter “41 of 154”; labels on 1931, 1937 and 2008

> An average is something markets drift toward over decades. It's not something they pay you each year.

*On screen:* Bars dim, and a rolling 10-year compound return line draws across

*Pronounced:* S&P 500 → S and P five hundred


## 4. The average isn't what you earn

> Averages also flatter. Say a hundred dollars gains fifty percent, then loses fifty percent.

*On screen:* $100 → $150 → $75 bars

> The average of those two years is zero. But you're left with seventy-five dollars.

*On screen:* “Average return: 0%” / “Your money: −25%”

> Since 1928, the average year returned about twelve percent. That's where Ramsey's number comes from.

*On screen:* Horizontal bar: average year since 1928, 11.9%, beside a “Ramsey's 12%” marker

> Compounded, money actually grew ten percent a year. After inflation, under seven.

*On screen:* Bars for compound growth (10.0%) and after inflation (6.8%)


## 5. One retiree: 1966

> Now let's follow one person. They saved ten thousand dollars a year, in today's money, from 1936 to 1965.

*On screen:* The savings line climbs from 1936 to 1965, with a year label at its tip

> They retired in 1966 with about two million dollars, and took eight percent: a hundred and fifty-nine thousand a year.

*On screen:* A vertical line at January 1966 and the $1.99M nest egg; pill “8% = $159K a year”

> Then came the worst stretch they could have drawn. Inflation, a flat market, and the crash of 1973 and 74.

*On screen:* The drawdown line falls in red while yearly real returns appear underneath (1973 −21%, 1974 −34%)

> By 1977, eleven years in, the money was gone.

*On screen:* An ✕ at the end of the line: “Broke in 1977”


## 6. Same returns, backwards

> Here's the strange part. Take the exact same thirty years of returns and play them in reverse order.

*On screen:* The real-order balance line, plus 30 return bars that slide into reverse order

> Same years. Same average. Same compound growth, about five percent a year after inflation.

*On screen:* Pill: “Same average: +5.0% a year”

> This time the good years come first, and our retiree finishes with one point six million dollars.

*On screen:* The reversed-order line draws to $1.63M

> The only thing that changed was the order. That's sequence of returns risk.

*On screen:* “Only the order changed.”


## 7. Everyone, every year

> So run the same plan for everyone who retired in every year from 1902 to 1996.

*On screen:* All 125 savers' paths draw in, gray at first

> Blue lines made it thirty years. Red lines ran dry.

*On screen:* Colors switch on: blue lasted, red ran out, gray still running

> At eight percent, forty-nine of ninety-five ran out of money.

*On screen:* Counter: “Ran out at 8%: 49 of 95”

> The worst, retiring in 1929, was broke in under nine years. And ten people who retired between 1997 and 2007 have already run out.

*On screen:* The 1929 path is highlighted (“broke in 8.6 years”), and a note that 10 people who retired 1997–2007 are already out


## 8. At four percent

> Here's each retirement as a bar, showing how many years the money lasted.

*On screen:* A bar per retirement year showing years lasted at 8%, all stocks

> Now drop to four percent, with half the money in bonds, the kind of mix Bill Bengen studied when he found the four percent rule.

*On screen:* The bars morph to 4% with half bonds; the counter goes from 46 to 91 of 95

> Ninety-one of ninety-five made it. The four that didn't all retired in the late sixties, and lasted at least twenty-five years.

*On screen:* Dots mark the four failures, labeled “1965, 1966, 1968, 1969: out after 25+ years”

> With all stocks, success slides fast past four and a half percent, and eight percent is a coin flip. With half in bonds, eight percent fails four times in five.

*On screen:* Crossfade to success-rate curves for all stocks and half bonds, marking 4%: 97%, 8%: 48% and 8%: 19%


## 9. The first decade decides

> Why did some fail? Not because their thirty-year average was bad.

*On screen:* A scatter: first-10-year real return across, 30-year real return up

> The 1935 retiree's money grew nine and a half percent a year and still ran out. The 1955 retiree made it on four and a half.

*On screen:* 1935 (ran out, +9.5%/yr) and 1955 (lasted, +4.6%/yr) circled, with guide lines

> What matters is the first ten years, while the balance is biggest and every withdrawal bites hardest.

*On screen:* The x-axis title is emphasized

> No one whose first decade averaged under three and a half percent survived. No one above eleven ran out.

*On screen:* Shaded zones: under +3.6%, none survived; over +10.8%, none ran out


## 10. Expensive markets, smaller paychecks

> The best warning sign has been how expensive stocks were on the day you retired.

*On screen:* A scatter of CAPE on retirement day against the highest sustainable rate, with 8% and 4% lines

> This is Shiller's CAPE ratio: stock prices divided by ten years of earnings. Up is the highest spending rate each retirement could have sustained.

*On screen:* The axis titles are emphasized

> Every retirement that could support eight percent began with a CAPE of twenty-one and a half or lower.

*On screen:* Everything above a CAPE of 21.4 is shaded: “none could sustain 8%”

> As of November 2026, it's thirty-nine. Only 1999 and 2000 started higher.

*On screen:* A “Nov 2026: 39.4” line slides in; 1999 and 2000 are marked on the axis

*Pronounced:* CAPE → cape


## 11. Fees come first

> Ramsey also steers people to actively managed funds, often sold with a front-end sales charge.

*On screen:* A 95-dot grid: index funds, 49 ran out

> Add a typical charge on each deposit, plus about two-thirds of a percent a year in fund expenses.

*On screen:* A second grid for A-share fees; median sales charges $9.9K

> At eight percent, failures climb from forty-nine to fifty-seven out of ninety-five.

*On screen:* The 8 retirements that flip from lasted to ran out pulse red; 57 of 95


## 12. Flexible spending

> You can make running out impossible. Just spend eight percent of whatever is left each year.

*On screen:* “Spend 8% of whatever is left” and a “never runs out” pill

> But your paycheck moves with the market. Each line is one retiree's income, compared with their first year.

*On screen:* Each retiree's income line, relative to their first year

> In the typical retirement, the leanest year paid less than half of what they started with.

*On screen:* A dot at each line's leanest year, and a median line at 48%


## 13. Plan for the sequence

> Twelve percent is an average. A retirement doesn't get the average. It gets one sequence of years, in one order.

*On screen:* “12% is an average. A retirement gets one sequence.”

> Four percent has survived almost every sequence on record. Eight percent survived about half.

*On screen:* Cards: 4% with half bonds, 91 of 95 lasted; 8% with all stocks, 46 of 95

> Try your own numbers in the simulator.

*On screen:* “Try your own numbers in the simulator” and “Historical simulation, not financial advice”
