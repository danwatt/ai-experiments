# Spending $80,000 a year from $1 million: script

The source of truth is [narration.json](narration.json). A $1,000,000 nest egg in the S&P 500 (100% stocks, no fees) withdraws 8% of the starting amount at the end of each year, with retirements starting every year from 1945 on and a 30-year horizon.

After chapter 2 the viewer chooses how to count. **Plain dollars**: the same $80,000 check every year, with nominal balances. **Today's dollars**: $80,000 of November 2026 buying power every year, raised with inflation. The **Dollars** toggle switches tracks at any time. Voiced with Kokoro (Heart) through Voicebox; each path runs about 3½ minutes.


# Shared chapters


## 1. The 8% promise

> You've saved a million dollars, and it's time to retire. How much can you spend each year?

*On screen:* “Your nest egg on retirement day: $1,000,000”

> Ramsey Solutions' answer is about eight percent. On a million dollars, that's eighty thousand a year.

*On screen:* “Ramsey: take about 8% a year · $80,000 a year”

> Their reasoning: good growth stock funds average ten to twelve percent over the long run, so your investments keep replacing what you take, and the extra covers inflation.

*On screen:* “10–12% fund growth − 8% what you spend = the rest covers inflation”, built one term at a time

> Most retirement research lands closer to four percent. That's forty thousand a year.

*On screen:* Pill: “Retirement research: about 4%, or $40,000 a year”


## 2. In Ramsey's world

> Here's Ramsey's world. Stocks return twelve percent every single year, and prices rise four percent.

*On screen:* Chart caption “+12% every year, prices +4% every year”

> You take eighty thousand the first year, and give yourself a four percent raise every year after that.

*On screen:* The balance line starts climbing; withdrawal bars grow from $80,000 by 4% a year

> Thirty years later the balance has grown to three point two million, which buys what one million did on day one. It never runs out.

*On screen:* The balance reaches $3.24M, “buys what $1M did on day one”; the last withdrawal is $249K

> But real markets don't move at one steady speed, and the order of the good and bad years matters. So from here, choose how to count. Plain dollars, the same eighty thousand dollar check every year. Or today's dollars, eighty thousand dollars of buying power every year, raised with inflation.

*On screen:* Two pills for the paths appear, and when the scene ends the player pauses with a choice: **Plain dollars** or **Today's dollars**


# Today's dollars track


## 3. Counting in today's dollars

> In today's dollars, the nest egg is a million dollars at November 2026 prices, and you spend eighty thousand dollars of that buying power every year, no matter what the market does.

*On screen:* “Today's dollars: November 2026”, “$1,000,000 at November 2026 prices”, “$80,000 of that buying power, every year”, “no matter what the market does”

> That's the lifestyle Ramsey's math promises: steady, and never running out.

*On screen:* “$80,000 a year, forever?”, “the lifestyle Ramsey's math promises”


## 4. Retire in January 2000

> Say you retired in January 2000, at the top of the dot-com boom.

> The market fell three years in a row. Your eighty thousand still came out every year, from a shrinking pile.

*On screen:* The line continues through the 2008 bar (−37%) to zero: ✕ “Broke in 2009”


## 5. Same years, backwards

> Here's the strange part. From 2000 through 2025, stocks averaged more than five percent a year after inflation.

*On screen:* The 26 yearly return bars slide into reverse order

*On screen:* The reversed line draws to $980K after 26 years

*On screen:* “Only the order changed.”

## 8. Another way: 8% of what's left

> There's another way to read eight percent: take eight percent of whatever is in the account at the start of each year.

*On screen:* “Take 8% of whatever is there, every year.”

> That can never run out. But now your paycheck rises and falls with the market.

*On screen:* The 2000 retiree's yearly checks in today's dollars, against a dashed “The plan: $80,000” line; pill “Never runs out: $439K left in 2025”

> Here's how it works. In January 2000 you have a million dollars and take out eighty thousand. The other nine hundred and twenty thousand stays invested. Stocks lose nine percent that year, and prices rise about three percent. So you start 2001 with about eight hundred and nine thousand in today's dollars, and eight percent of that is about sixty-five thousand.

*On screen:* A ledger card builds row by row: January 2000 $1,000,000 → take 8% −$80,000 → left invested $920,000 → 2000: stocks −9.0%, prices +3.4% → January 2001 $809,488 in today's dollars → take 8% −$64,759; the 2000 and 2001 bars appear

> Keep going, and after the 2008 crash, the 2009 check was twenty-two thousand.

*On screen:* The card clears and the rest of the bars fill in: “2009, after the 2008 crash: $22K, down 42%”; hint “Point at any bar for that year's money movements”

> From then on it never topped thirty-eight thousand. Planned for eighty thousand, living on about thirty.

*On screen:* “2009–2025: never above $38K”

## 10. Why the first years matter

> Why does the order matter so much? Because every withdrawal takes a bigger bite out of a smaller pile.

*On screen:* “A bigger bite out of a smaller pile”

> For the 2000 retiree, the first year's eighty thousand was about nine percent of the balance. By 2002 it was seventeen percent. By 2006, a quarter of what was left.

*On screen:* Bars of the year's $80,000 as a share of the balance, 2000–2008: 9%, 17% in 2002, 25% in 2006, 77% in 2008

> Money spent after a crash isn't there for the recovery.

*On screen:* “Spent after a crash, it isn't there for the recovery.”


## 11. What history says

> Ramsey's eight percent works when the early years are kind. In today's dollars, since 1945, it failed about four times in ten.

*On screen:* Card: “8%: $80,000 of buying power: 31 of 52 lasted”

> Four percent isn't exciting, but it survived almost every start date on record.

*On screen:* Card: “4%: $40,000 of buying power: 50 of 52 lasted”

> The long-run average doesn't pay your bills. The order of the years decides.

*On screen:* “The long-run average doesn't pay your bills. The order of the years decides.” and “Historical simulation, 100% stocks, no fees or taxes. Not financial advice.”
