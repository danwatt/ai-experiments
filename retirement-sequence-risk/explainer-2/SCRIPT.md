# Ben and Arthur, a generation apart: script

The source of truth is [narration.json](narration.json). Ben is the father and Arthur his son. Both follow the plan Ramsey's chart gives Arthur: $2,000 a year from age 27 through 65, 39 deposits, which at 12% reaches the chart's $1,532,166. Their windows are 25 years apart, and only windows starting from 1945 on are used.

After chapter 2 the viewer chooses how to count. **Plain dollars** uses a flat $2,000 and nominal balances, as in Ramsey's chart. **Today's dollars** raises deposits with inflation and shows balances at November 2026 prices. The **Dollars** toggle in the player switches tracks at any time. Voiced with Kokoro (Heart) through Voicebox; each path runs about 4 minutes.


# Shared chapters


## 1. Ben and Arthur

> Ramsey Solutions likes to tell a story about two investors, Ben and Arthur, to show the power of compound growth.

*On screen:* Ben (the father) and Arthur avatars appear

> Here's a different version. Ben is the father. Arthur is his son, born when Ben was twenty-five.

*On screen:* Labels: “the father”, “his son”

> Each puts two thousand dollars a year into the stock market, from age twenty-seven through sixty-five, just like Arthur in Ramsey's chart. Thirty-nine years. Same plan, same money.

*On screen:* Two rows of 39 $2,000 blocks fill in, age 27 to 65

> The only difference is which thirty-nine years they get. Arthur starts twenty-five years after his father.

*On screen:* Arthur's row slides 25 years right on a calendar line; the overlap is marked “14 years in common”


## 2. In a twelve percent world

> If the market returned twelve percent every year, it wouldn't matter when they started.

*On screen:* Two smooth 12%-a-year curves, Arthur's starting 25 years later

> Each would retire with about one point five three million dollars, the same number Ramsey's chart gives Arthur.

*On screen:* Both end at $1.53M (Ramsey's chart: $1,532,166): “Both retire with $1.53M”

> But real markets don't run at one speed, and prices don't stand still. So from here, you choose. Follow the story in plain dollars, the way Ramsey's chart counts them, or in today's dollars, adjusted for inflation.

*On screen:* Pills for the two paths appear, and when the scene ends the player pauses with a choice: **Plain dollars** or **Today's dollars**


# Today's dollars track


## 3. Counting in today's dollars

> Today means November 2026. Each deposit keeps the buying power of two thousand dollars at November 2026 prices, and every balance is shown in those same dollars.

*On screen:* “Today's dollars: November 2026”, “Each deposit buys what $2,000 bought in November 2026”, and “Every balance is shown in those same dollars”

> Ramsey's twelve percent, less four percent inflation, works out to about four hundred and seventy-six thousand.

*On screen:* “Ramsey's 12% − 4% inflation: $476K after 39 years of $2,000 a year”


## 4. Ben from 1957, Arthur from 1982

> Say Ben invests from 1957 through 1995. His son Arthur invests from 1982 through 2020.

*On screen:* Ben's line (1957–1995) draws by calendar year; Arthur's (1982–2020) joins in 1982

> In the money of the time, Ben's yearly deposit grew from about a hundred and sixty dollars in 1957 to almost nine hundred by 1995. Arthur's went from about five hundred and fifty dollars to about fifteen hundred.

*On screen:* Top right: “Each year's deposit in the dollars of the time”, “Ben: $163 in 1957 → $886 in 1995” and “Arthur: $556 in 1982 → $1,520 in 2020”

> Ben's money spent its middle years in the flat seventies. He retires in 1995 with three hundred and fifty-six thousand dollars.

*On screen:* Shaded eras with real returns; “Ben: $356K”

> Arthur's biggest years caught two booms, the nineties and the 2010s. He retires in 2020 with five hundred and forty-six thousand. Fifty-three percent more, from the same plan.

*On screen:* “Arthur: $546K” and the pill “Same plan: Arthur has 53% more”

## 6. Every 39 years since 1945

> Here's every thirty-nine-year window that started between 1945 and 1987. Each bar is what the same two thousand dollars a year grew to.

*On screen:* 43 bars, one per 39-year window starting 1945–1987, in today's dollars (November 2026)

> The best started in 1961 and ended at the 1999 peak, with six hundred and sixty-six thousand. The worst started in 1946 and ended in 1984, with two hundred and sixty-one thousand. One year can matter too: start in 1969 and it's four hundred and forty-three thousand. Start in 1970, ending in 2008, and it's two hundred and seventy-one.

*On screen:* Best (1961, ended 1999: $666K), worst (1946, ended 1984: $261K), then 1969: $443K beside 1970: $271K

> Ramsey's twelve percent math says four hundred and seventy-six thousand. Only ten of these forty-three windows got there. None fell below half, so their hedge held, but most came up short.

*On screen:* Ramsey's dashed $476K line (it stays to the end) and a half line at $238K; counter “Reached Ramsey's number: 10 of 43”

> Pair every father with a son twenty-five years later, and the son came out ahead thirteen times out of eighteen. In eleven of those pairs, one ended with at least a quarter more than the other.

*On screen:* Counter “Son ahead of father: 13 of 18 · One at least 25% ahead: 11”


## 7. Why the late years decide it

> What decides it? Not the early years. A young saver's first deposits are small.

*On screen:* Money invested each year for Ben (1961–1999) and Arthur (1986–2024), over a strip of yearly real returns

> What matters is the market in the last fifteen or twenty years, when most of the money is invested.

*On screen:* Each one's last 20 years are highlighted

> Ben's biggest balances rode the boom of the eighties and nineties, and he retired at the top. Arthur's had to sit through 2008.

*On screen:* Shaded eras: flat 1966–82, boom 1983–99, lost decade 2000–08, recovery 2009–21

> It's sequence of returns risk, at the end of saving instead of the start of spending.

*On screen:* “Sequence risk, at the end of saving”


## 8. Same plan, different decades

> Both of them did everything right. They started at twenty-seven and never missed a year.

*On screen:* Avatars and “Started at 27. Never missed a year.”

> The difference wasn't discipline. It was which decades they got.

*On screen:* “Same plan. Different decades.” Since 1945 the son ended with up to 82% more (father from 1956, son from 1981)

> A plan built on twelve percent every year assumes every stretch of years is the same. History says otherwise.

*On screen:* “12% every year assumes every stretch is the same. History says otherwise.”
