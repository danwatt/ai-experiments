The Total Money Makeover, checked against history

What if I’m half wrong?

Dave Ramsey asks this himself, right after telling a young couple they could retire with almost $8 million. His retirement plan rests on a few round numbers. Here is how each one held up against real history.

What the book says

“How much do you need to retire with dignity and security? How long will it take you to get there? … You are secure and will leave a nice inheritance when you can live off of 8 percent of your nest egg per year. If you make 12 percent on your money [on] average and inflation steals 4 percent, 8 percent is a dream number. If you make 12 percent and only pull out 8 percent, you grow your nest egg by 4 percent per year. That 4 percent keeps your nest egg, and therefore your income, ahead of inflation ’til death do you part. You get a cost-of-living raise from your nest egg every year. If you can live with dignity on $40,000, you need a nest egg of only $500,000.”

Dave Ramsey, The Total Money Makeover, chapter 9 (first published 2003). Bold added; the ellipsis marks a cut.

Do prices rise 4% a year?

Over the last 100 years, prices rose about 3% a year. They ran hotter in the 1970s and early '80s, and cooler since the book came out.

If anything, 4% is a little high, which is the safe side to miss on. Prices rose 3% a year before the book and 2.6% a year since.

More detail

Prices rose more than 4% in : 12 of them from 1968 to 1981, six around World War II and the Korean War, and only three since the book (2007, 2021 and 2022). Over the 30 years just before the book, which held the 1970s, prices rose 4.9% a year, so 4% looked reasonable at the time.

Inflation here is the December to December change in the Consumer Price Index (CPI-U): Robert Shiller's monthly CPI for 1926–1927 and Aswath Damodaran's series (NYU Stern) from 1928, the same figures the simulator uses. Averages are compounded, the steady yearly rate that gets prices from start to end. Hover a year on the chart for its figures.

First, a quick aside

Two ways to average a return

Simple average

Add up each year's return and divide by the number of years. This is the 12% Ramsey quotes.

Compounded

The steady yearly rate your money actually grew at, start to finish. Also called CAGR.

They aren't the same. Here is $10,000 over four years that average 12%:

YearReturnYour balanceAt a steady 12%
Start$10,000$10,000
1+30%$13,000$11,200
2−10%$11,700$12,544
3+25%$14,625$14,049
4+3%$15,064$15,735
Simple average12.0%
Compounded10.8%

Same 12% average, but the money only grew as if it earned 10.8% a year. Losses hurt more than gains help: lose 10% and you need 11% just to get back. Over a long time the gap is huge. From 1926 to 2002 the S&P 500 averaged 11.9% but compounded at 10.0%, and $10,000 became $15 million instead of $59 million.

The math

Simple average: (30 − 10 + 25 + 3) ÷ 4 = 12.0%. Compounded: (ending ÷ starting)1 ÷ years − 1 = ($15,064 ÷ $10,000)1 ÷ 4 − 1 = 10.8%. The more the years swing, the further the compounded rate falls below the average; with steady returns the two are equal. In the extreme, +50% then −50% averages 0%, but $10,000 becomes $15,000 and then $7,500. For the book's math to work, a nest egg has to actually grow 12% a year, which means the compounded figure has to be 12%.

Do stocks earn 12% a year?

As a simple average, yes: the S&P 500 averaged about 12% a year both before the book and since. But money grows at the compounded rate, which was lower: 10% a year before the book and 11.3% since.

“What if I’m half wrong?” Saving $625 a month

The title of this page comes from a passage in the book where a young couple saves for retirement:

“If Joe and Suzy invest $625 per month with no match into Roth IRAs from age thirty to age seventy, they will have $7,588,545 tax-FREE! That is almost $8 million. What if I’m half wrong? What if you end up with only $4 million? What if I’m six times wrong? Sure beats the 97 out of 100 sixty-five-year-olds who can’t write a check for $600!”

Dave Ramsey, The Total Money Makeover. Bold added.

That is $625 a month for 40 years, $300,000 in all. Here is what it actually became over every 40-year stretch in the record, against the book's figure, its “half wrong” $4 million, and “six times wrong,” a sixth of the figure, about $1.26 million.

Deposit

Raising the deposit 4% a year, so it keeps up with the book's inflation, means putting in $712,691 instead of $300,000; by the last year it is $2,885 a month. Then every stretch reached $4 million, and the four funds reached the book's $7.59 million in 2 of 6, saving from 1981 and 1982. In the money of the year they started saving, the balances were worth $1.7 to $2.8 million, still about a third of the book's figure.

None came close to $7.6 million. Most landed near the book's “half wrong” $4 million: the four funds reached it in 5 of 6 stretches, the S&P 500 in 5 and VFINX in 4. But those are dollars 40 years later, after prices roughly tripled. In the money of the year they started saving, the balances were worth $1.2 to $2 million, four to six times less than the book's figure.

◐Closer, at a cost raising the $625 by 4% a year

In plain dollars, every stretch ended above the book's “half wrong” $4 million, and the four funds reached $7.59 million twice. But that took $712,691 of savings instead of $300,000, and after inflation the balances were worth $1.7 to $2.8 million, still three to four times short of the book's figure.

◐Half wrong, as the book allows $625 a month becoming $7.6 million

In plain dollars, every stretch ended near the book's “half wrong” $4 million, never near $7.6 million. After inflation, it was closer to “six times wrong.” Still far better than not saving at all.

More detail

Even a perfectly steady 12% a year turns $625 a month for 40 years into about $6.1 million, not $7.6 million. The book's figure matches growth of 1% a month, which compounds to 12.7% a year. Raised 4% a year, a steady 12% makes about $8.8 million.

With the funds' record starting in 1981, there are only six 40-year stretches, ending from 2020 to 2025. Tick the box to add the S&P 500 back to 1926: of the 55 stretches that began from 1926 to 1980, 2 reached $7.59 million (saving from 1959 and 1960, ending at the 1999 peak), 45 reached $4 million, and all beat $1.26 million. Raising the deposit 4% a year, 20 of the 55 reached $7.59 million and all reached $4 million.

Deposits come at the start of each month, and each year's return is spread evenly over its months. Fund returns are after their yearly costs but not the Class A sales charge, which would leave about 4% less. Roth IRAs began in 1998, and $7,500 a year is more than early IRA limits allowed; like the book, this ignores both, and taxes.

Can you spend 8% a year?

The book's math only works if the whole year's 12% arrives before any money is spent. Real retirees spend through the year, like a paycheck. Even in a perfect world, with exactly 12% returns and 4% inflation every single year, that matters.

Take $40,000 a year from $500,000, paid monthly ($3,333 a month) and raised 4% each year. To keep up, the balance has to grow 4% a year too, to $520,000 after year one. Spent monthly, it only grows about 3.5%, and that small gap adds up.

  • Spent monthly, 8% runs out in year 38.
  • Taken all on January 1, it runs out in year 31.
  • The most you can spend monthly and keep up is 7.52%, about $37,600 a year. To spend $40,000, you would need about $532,000, not $500,000.

✕Falls short spending 8% a year

Even with perfect years, spending 8% as a monthly paycheck runs out in year 38. The safe number is closer to 7.5%. The gap is small, but it shows up before a single bad year.

Numbers for each line

7.69% comes from dividing out inflation instead of subtracting it: 1.12 ÷ 1.04 = 1.0769, a real return of 7.69%. Spent monthly, it lasts to year 52 but still slowly falls behind.

The math behind 7.52%

If the whole year's 12% landed before any money came out, the check could be 8% of the balance: 12% growth − 8% out = 4% left, just enough to raise both by 4%. Spent monthly, each payment leaves early and misses the growth it would have earned by year end: January's misses 12 months of it, December's one month. On average a dollar paid monthly would have grown 6.39% by year end, so paying it monthly costs the balance as much as paying 1.0639 times as much at year end. The most it can pay is 8% ÷ 1.0639 = 7.52%.

The 1.0639: with m = 1.121/12 − 1 = 0.949% a month, a payment at the start of month k grows (1 + m)13−k by year end, and the twelve average to (1 + m) × ((1 + m)12 − 1) ÷ (12m) = 1.0639. All on January 1 misses the whole 12%: 8% ÷ 1.12 = 7.14%.

Other ways to make monthly spending last at a steady 12%, each just enough on its own: earn 12.5% a year instead of 12% (counting 12% as “1% a month” does it, since that compounds to 12.7%), or raise the check 3.5% a year instead of 4%, which lasts but lets the income fall behind inflation.

What actually happened

Now real history. Each retiree starts with $500,000, spends $40,000 the first year as a monthly paycheck, and gets a raise each January equal to the actual inflation of the year before. The money earns what the S&P 500, VFINX or Ramsey's four funds actually returned.

A retirement keeps up if, at the end, the nest egg still buys at least what $500,000 did at the start, as the book describes.

More detail

Over 30 years at $40,000, the S&P 500 kept up in 29 of 71 retirements and ran out in 35, including every one that began from 1926 to 1931 and all but one from 1956 to 1977. The four funds kept up in all 16 thirty-year retirements, but those all began from 1981 to 1996. Of the ten 30-year retirements that began from 1997 to 2006, nine have already run out on the S&P 500 and on VFINX, and eight on the four funds. Every retirement that began from 2004 to 2008 has run out, except the 20-year retirement on the four funds from 2004, which lasted but shrank. Every one from 2009 to 2013 is on track so far. Spending 7.52% saves a few of the 2004 to 2008 retirements from running out, but none of them keeps up.

The 7.52% choice is the most that keeps up in the perfect world of the section above.
