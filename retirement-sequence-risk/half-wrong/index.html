The Total Money Makeover, checked against history

What if I’m half wrong?

Dave Ramsey asks it himself, right after telling a young couple they could have almost $8 million for retirement. His retirement math rests on a few round numbers. This page takes them one at a time and holds each up against what stocks, inflation and his own recommended funds actually did.

What the book says

“How much do you need to retire with dignity and security? How long will it take you to get there? … You are secure and will leave a nice inheritance when you can live off of 8 percent of your nest egg per year. If you make 12 percent on your money [on] average and inflation steals 4 percent, 8 percent is a dream number. If you make 12 percent and only pull out 8 percent, you grow your nest egg by 4 percent per year. That 4 percent keeps your nest egg, and therefore your income, ahead of inflation ’til death do you part. You get a cost-of-living raise from your nest egg every year. If you can live with dignity on $40,000, you need a nest egg of only $500,000.”

Dave Ramsey, The Total Money Makeover, chapter 9 (first published 2003). Bold added; the ellipsis marks a cut.

Does inflation steal 4 percent?

US consumer prices, the last 100 years. Before the book, prices had risen about 3% a year over the long run, though closer to 5% over the 30 years just before it, which held the inflation of the 1970s and early '80s. Since the book they have risen about 2½% a year. Prices rose more than 4% in : 12 of them from 1968 to 1981, six around World War II and the Korean War, and only three since the book (2007, 2021 and 2022).

Aside

Average year or compounded: two ways to say “12% a year”

Average year simple or arithmetic average

Add up each year's return and divide by the number of years. This is how Ramsey states the 12%, and how most “historical average” figures are quoted.

Compounded CAGR, compound annual growth rate

The one steady yearly rate that would turn the starting balance into the ending balance: (ending ÷ starting)1 ÷ years − 1. This is what the money actually grew at.

Same 12% average, but the balance ends $671 short of a steady 12%: it grew as if it had made 10.8% every year. A loss costs more than the same-sized gain makes back, because the gain then works on a smaller balance. The extreme case: +50% then −50% averages 0%, but $10,000 becomes $15,000 and then $7,500. The more the years swing, the further the compounded rate falls below the average; with steady returns the two are the same.

Over decades the gap adds up. From 1926 to 2002 the S&P 500 averaged 11.9% a year but compounded at 10.0%. $10,000 put in at the start of 1926 grew to $15.2 million by the end of 2002; a steady 11.9% a year would have made it $59.3 million. Ramsey's math, a nest egg that grows 12% a year, needs the compounded figure to be 12%.

Do stocks make 12 percent on average?

Ramsey points to the S&P 500's historical average. Add up each year’s return and divide, and it was 11.9% a year from 1926 to 2002, 12% in round numbers, so as a simple average his figure holds, and it has held since the book. But a balance grows at the compound rate, which ups and downs pull lower: 10.0% a year before the book and 11.3% since. His four recommended funds did better before the book than after it; at the end of 2002 their own printed lifetime returns ran from 11.3% to 15.2% a year.

Can you live off 8 percent a year?

The book works a year at a time: make 12%, take out 8%, and the 4% left over keeps up with inflation. Nowhere does it specify when the money comes out.

A simple reading is a yearly budget: $40,000 a year from $500,000, paid out monthly like a paycheck to cover bills and expenses, $3,333 a month, and raised 4% each January. But, as we will see in a moment, this falls short.

An alternate reading would be to take out the entire $40,000 at the start of the year, put into a savings account, and leave $460,000 to grow. This falls even further short, and even under ideal conditions runs out after 31 years.

In order to succeed under the Ramsey advice, we need to end the year not at $500,000, but rather at that plus 4% to keep pace with inflation: $520,000.

Four ways to draw the money, every year exactly 12% and 4%

Dollars

Drawn monthly like a paycheck, 8% runs out in year 38. Dividing out inflation instead of subtracting it, 12% ÷ 4% (1.12 ÷ 1.04), gives a real return of 7.69%; drawn monthly, that lasts to year 52 but still slowly falls behind. Drawn monthly, the rate that exactly keeps up is 7.52%, and living on $40,000 then takes about $532,000, not $500,000.

Where 7.52% comes from

If the whole year's 12% landed before any money came out, a check could be 8% of the balance: 12% growth − 8% out = 4% left, just enough to raise both by 4%. Drawn monthly, each payment leaves before the year is over and misses the growth it would have earned by year end: the January payment misses 12 months of it, the December payment one month. On average a dollar paid monthly would have grown 6.39% by year end. So paying W monthly costs the balance as much as paying W × 1.0639 at year end, and the most it can pay is

8% ÷ 1.0639 = 7.52%

The 1.0639: with m = 1.121/12 − 1 = 0.949% a month, a payment at the start of month k grows (1 + m)13−k by year end, and the twelve average to (1 + m) × ((1 + m)12 − 1) ÷ (12m) = 1.0639. The same idea gives the others: all on January 1 misses the whole 12%, 8% ÷ 1.12 = 7.14%; 7.69% is 8% ÷ 1.04, which divides out inflation but not the timing.

Other ways to make monthly draws last at a steady 12%, each just enough on its own:

  • Earn 12.5% a year instead of 12%. Counting 12% as “1% a month” does it, because 1% a month compounds to 12.7% a year. That is a different return, not a different time to take the money out.
  • Raise the check 3.5% a year instead of 4%. The money lasts, but the income falls behind 4% inflation, so the “cost-of-living raise every year” is gone.

The book's “grow your nest egg by 4 percent per year” leaves no slack, so when the money comes out decides whether it lasts, before a single bad year. Real years are not steady, which the next section takes up.

✕Falls short the book's 8 percent

Even with exactly 12% returns and 4% inflation every year, drawing 8% like a paycheck runs out in year 38, and drawing it all on January 1 runs out in year 31. The most that keeps up when drawn monthly is 7.52%, so living on $40,000 takes about $532,000, not $500,000. The gap is small, but it opens before a single bad year.

What actually happened

Now the real record, with the book's numbers and the most generous way to draw them. Each retiree starts with $500,000 and takes $40,000 the first year in twelve monthly payments, the timing that keeps the most money invested. Each January the check rises by the actual inflation of the year before instead of a flat 4%, so it always buys what $40,000 did at the start; in the few years prices fell, it falls with them. The money earns what the S&P 500, VFINX or Ramsey's four funds actually returned that year.

The book says the nest egg stays ahead of inflation ’til death do you part, so a retirement keeps pace here only if it ends with at least $500,000 in the dollars of the year it began. The second choice below draws 7.52% instead, the rate that ends each year with enough for a raise in the book's steady world.

At $40,000, over 30 years the S&P 500 kept pace in 29 of 71 retirements and ran out in 35, including all but one that began from 1956 to 1977 and every one that began from 1926 to 1931. Ramsey's four funds kept pace in all 16 thirty-year retirements, but those all began from 1981 to 1996, at the start of the strongest run in the record; over 20 years, every retirement on the four funds that began from 1999 to 2002 ran out. Of the three 20-year retirements that began after the book, all ran out on the S&P 500 and VFINX, and two of three on the four funds. The 30-year retirements still underway, those that began from 1997 to 2006, look worse: by the end of 2025, nine of the ten had already run out on the S&P 500 and on VFINX, and eight of ten on the four funds.

In the ten years after the book, it comes down to 2008. Every retirement that began from 2004 to 2008 has already run out on the S&P 500 and VFINX, whether it was meant to last 20, 25 or 30 years, and so has every one on the four funds except the 20-year retirement from 2004, which lasted but shrank. Every retirement that began from 2009 to 2013, just after the crash, is on pace so far, carried by the long rise that followed. Drawing 7.52% saves a few of the 2004 to 2008 retirements from running out, but none of them keeps pace.

✕Falls short, depending on the year $40,000 from $500,000 in real markets

With real returns and real inflation, the book's plan worked for some retirees and failed for others, and the difference was mostly the year they began. On the S&P 500 about half of all 30-year retirements ran out of money, and fewer than half kept the nest egg ahead of inflation as the book describes. The four funds' perfect 30-year record covers only retirements that began from 1981 to 1996; of the ten that began from 1997 to 2006, eight have already run out. Every retirement that began in the five years after the book, 2004 to 2008, has run out or fallen behind; those that began just after the 2008 crash are on pace so far. Drawing 7.52% instead of 8% helps only a little.
