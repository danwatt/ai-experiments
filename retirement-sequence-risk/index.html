8% or 4%? Saving and spending through every year since 1872

Ramsey Solutions says a retiree can withdraw 8% a year: stocks average 12%, inflation takes 4%, and 8% is left to safely spend. The safe-withdrawal research says about 4%. This page runs both rules through real history. Each line is one person who saved for a set number of years, retired, and then drew a steady income from what they had: raised with inflation in today's dollars, or the same check every year in plain dollars. Some ran out of money and some did not, and the difference is mostly the order the returns arrived in.

How it works

  • Returns. Calendar-year total returns with dividends reinvested. S&P 500, 10-year Treasury and CPI inflation for 1928–2025 come from Damodaran's historical returns. 1872–1927 uses Shiller's monthly data (S&P Composite total return, GS10 bond return, CPI) taken December to December. Shiller's prices are monthly averages, so his early years are a little smoother than true year-end figures.
  • Today's dollars or plain dollars. The Dollars switch picks how to count, as in the explainers. Today's dollars (the default) converts every year's return to a real return, after inflation: deposits and the yearly check keep pace with prices, and every balance is at November 2026 prices, so a flat line means steady buying power. Plain dollars counts the way Ramsey's charts do: the same $2,000 deposit and the same dollar check every year, balances as the statements showed them, and Ramsey's line grows at 12% a year. Sales-charge breakpoints then apply to those statement balances. A flat check is easier on the portfolio, but each year it buys a little less.
  • Saving. The yearly deposit goes in at the start of each year, as in Ramsey's Ben and Arthur chart, and keeps pace with inflation. The defaults are that chart's Arthur: $2,000 a year from age 27 through 65, 39 deposits.
  • Spending. On retirement day the withdrawal rate sets a dollar income: 8% of $1M is $80,000. That income is raised with inflation every year no matter what the market did, the rule both Ramsey and the 4% research describe. Withdrawals come out at the end of each year, the timing under which Ramsey's arithmetic (12% − 4% = 8%) holds exactly. Taking them at the start of the year would make every result a little worse.
  • Spending rules. Fixed is the rule above. Skip raise holds income flat (losing ground to inflation) in any year the portfolio lost money. Guardrails follows Guyton and Klinger: the skip rule, plus a 10% pay cut whenever the current withdrawal is more than 20% above the starting rate (not in the last 15 years), and a 10% raise when it is more than 20% below. Same % withdraws the rate times whatever the balance is, so it never runs out but the income swings with the market. "Leanest year" is the smallest yearly income as a share of the first year's.
  • American Funds fees. Ramsey sends listeners to SmartVestor Pros and has defended paying front-end sales loads, and American Funds Class A shares are a common pick. The checkbox applies Capital Group's Class A schedule for stock and balanced funds: each deposit pays 5.75% if the deposit plus the account's current value is under $25,000, 5.00% from $25,000, 4.50% from $50,000, 3.50% from $100,000, 2.50% from $250,000, 2.00% from $500,000, 1.50% from $750,000, and nothing from $1 million (rights of accumulation). The whole deposit pays the one rate, and it comes off before the money is invested. Breakpoints are measured in whichever dollars the page is counting: today's dollars, or the statement balance in plain dollars. On top of that, 0.58% a year comes out of returns, the expense ratio of The Growth Fund of America Class A (0.25% management, 0.24% 12b-1, 0.09% other). Not modeled: the 1% charge on shares bought at $1 million or more and sold within 18 months.
  • CAPE (cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, also called the Shiller P/E) measures how expensive stocks were on retirement day: the S&P price in January of the retirement year divided by the average of the previous ten years of earnings, adjusted for inflation. Since 1881 it has averaged about 18. It was 27 in January 1929, 44 in January 2000, and 39 in November 2026. A high CAPE has tended to mean lower returns over the following decade, and so less room for a retiree's withdrawals. Shiller's figures start in 1881.
  • Safe max rate (often called SAFEMAX) is the largest first-year withdrawal, raised with inflation every year after, that would have left exactly $0 at the end of that retirement, at the chosen stock mix and fees. A retirement with a safe max of 6.2% would have survived any rate up to 6.2%.
  • Inheritance left is the balance after the last year of retirement, in today's dollars.
  • S&P 500: Standard & Poor's index of 500 large U.S. companies, the usual stand-in for "the stock market." Returns here include reinvested dividends. Before 1957 the figures come from earlier, smaller versions of the index (Shiller's S&P Composite).
  • CAGR (compound annual growth rate): the one steady yearly rate that turns the starting amount into the ending amount. Unlike a simple average of yearly returns, it counts the damage a loss does to everything after it.
  • CPI (Consumer Price Index): the government's measure of the prices people pay. Inflation here is its change from one year to the next, and "today's dollars" are dollars at November 2026 CPI.
  • GS10: the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield in Shiller's data, used to work out 10-year Treasury bond returns before 1928.
  • 12b-1 fee: a yearly marketing and distribution fee built into some funds' expense ratios, named for the SEC rule that allows it. It mostly pays the advisor who sold the fund.
  • Success means the balance never hit zero during the retirement years. Retirements that haven't finished yet are shown in gray and left out of the success rate, unless they've already run out.
  • Not modeled: taxes, Social Security, spending cuts in bad years, international stocks. Real portfolios with fees higher than an index fund do worse than the 0% setting.
