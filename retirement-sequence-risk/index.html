8% or 4%? Saving and spending through every year since 1872

Dave Ramsey says a retiree can take 8% a year: stocks average 12%, inflation takes 4%, and 8% is left to spend. The safe-withdrawal research says about 4%. This page runs both rules through real history. Each line is one person who saved for a set number of years, retired, and then drew a fixed, inflation-adjusted income from what they had. Some ran out of money and some did not, and the difference is mostly the order the returns arrived in.

How it works

  • Returns. Calendar-year total returns with dividends reinvested. S&P 500, 10-year Treasury and CPI inflation for 1928–2025 come from Damodaran's historical returns. 1872–1927 uses Shiller's monthly data (S&P Composite total return, GS10 bond return, CPI) taken December to December. Shiller's prices are monthly averages, so his early years are a little smoother than true year-end figures.
  • Everything is in today's dollars. Each year's return is converted to a real return (after inflation), so a flat line means steady purchasing power.
  • Saving. The yearly deposit lands at the end of each year and keeps pace with inflation.
  • Spending. On retirement day the withdrawal rate sets a dollar income: 8% of $1M is $80,000. That income is raised with inflation every year no matter what the market did, the rule both Ramsey and the 4% research describe. Withdrawals come out at the end of each year, the timing under which Ramsey's arithmetic (12% − 4% = 8%) holds exactly. Taking them at the start of the year would make every result a little worse.
  • Spending rules. Fixed is the rule above. Skip raise holds income flat (losing ground to inflation) in any year the portfolio lost money. Guardrails follows Guyton and Klinger: the skip rule, plus a 10% pay cut whenever the current withdrawal is more than 20% above the starting rate (not in the last 15 years), and a 10% raise when it is more than 20% below. Same % withdraws the rate times whatever the balance is, so it never runs out but the income swings with the market. "Leanest year" is the smallest yearly income as a share of the first year's.
  • Fees "as practiced". Ramsey sends listeners to SmartVestor Pros and has defended paying front-end sales loads; A-share funds commonly charge up to 5.75% of each deposit, falling at breakpoints as the account grows (5.00% from $25K, 4.50% from $50K, 3.50% from $100K, 2.50% from $250K, 2.00% from $500K, 1.50% from $750K, none from $1M; here measured in today's dollars against the account balance). The preset pairs that with a 0.65% expense ratio, about the average for actively managed stock funds. The load comes off each deposit before it is invested.
  • Valuation. CAPE is Shiller's cyclically adjusted P/E: the S&P price divided by ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings, taken in January of the retirement year (from 1881). The highest sustainable rate (SAFEMAX) is the largest fixed, inflation-adjusted withdrawal that would have left exactly $0 at the end, at the chosen stock mix and fees.
  • Success means the balance never hit zero during the retirement years. Retirements that haven't finished yet are shown in gray and left out of the success rate, unless they've already run out.
  • Not modeled: taxes, Social Security, spending cuts in bad years, international stocks. Real portfolios with fees higher than an index fund do worse than the 0% setting.
