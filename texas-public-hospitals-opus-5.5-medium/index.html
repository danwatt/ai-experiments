Where the money goes at Texas' public hospitals

Texas has about 100 hospitals owned by a county, a city or a hospital district. Six big urban systems take in most of the money: Parkland, Harris Health, University Health, JPS, UMC Lubbock and UMC El Paso. These charts show ten years of their revenue and expenses as filed with Medicare, and how much of it the local property tax pays for.

    What the audits say

    The Medicare cost reports above cover the hospital. Each district's audited financial statements cover the whole district and split the year into two parts: running the hospitals (operating result) and everything else, mostly property tax and investment income. Five of the big six lose money running their hospitals every year. At Parkland, Harris Health, University Health and JPS, property tax more than covers that loss, and investment income adds to the surplus. UMC El Paso's tax roughly covers it. UMC Lubbock is different: its hospitals mostly break even or make money, and it levies very little tax. Select a row to see that system year by year.

    About the data

    Every number here comes from the Medicare cost report each hospital files every year, as published by CMS in its Hospital Provider Cost Report files.

    How to read it
    • Cost reports cover the hospital, not the whole district. Audited district statements can differ. They may count clinics, health plans (Parkland Community Health Plan, JPS's and Harris Health's Medicaid HMOs) and tax collections that the hospital's cost report leaves out or books differently.
    • Fiscal years. Each report is labeled with the year its midpoint falls in, so an October–September year ending September 2024 is FY2024. Harris Health ran March–February until 2022, then filed a 7-month report (March–September 2022) to move to an October year. Its FY2022 is short, and the charts mark it.
    • Property tax comes from the Texas Comptroller's yearly Special District Rates and Levies reports (tax years 2013 to 2025; the years before 2021 are no longer linked from the Comptroller's site and come from the Internet Archive). It is the levy, taxable value times the adopted rate. Hospitals actually collect slightly less, usually 97 to 99%. Tax year Y is billed in October and due January 31 of Y+1, so it is matched to the fiscal year that contains that date: tax year 2023 pays for FY2024. Harris Health's 7-month FY2022 contains no January 31, so it shows no levy. A few years where a district's reported rate is off by a factor of 3 or more from its usual rate, almost always a keying error, are left blank.
    • Other income is everything outside patient care: property taxes, grants, investment income, and for some hospitals supplemental Medicaid money. In the detail chart, the levy is taken out of other income and shown on its own. Where the levy is larger than the reported other income (Midland in some years), the hospital books part of its tax elsewhere and the bars add up to more than reported revenue. The tooltip says so. Hospitals book supplemental payments inconsistently. Some count them as patient revenue, so the split between the two varies from place to place.
    • Rural districts with nursing homes. Many small hospital districts own or lease nursing homes to qualify them for Texas Medicaid's nursing facility supplemental programs (QIPP and earlier MPAP). That money moves through the hospital's books, so a 25-bed hospital can show hundreds of millions in revenue and expenses. Uvalde, Coryell and Limestone are examples. Pages for those hospitals carry a flag.
    • Not included: state-owned hospitals (UT Southwestern, UTMB, MD Anderson, UT Tyler, the state psychiatric hospitals) and hospital districts that run no hospital of their own, such as Central Health in Travis County (which funds care at Dell Seton) and the Nueces County Hospital District (which funds CHRISTUS Spohn).
    • Dollars are nominal, not adjusted for inflation.
